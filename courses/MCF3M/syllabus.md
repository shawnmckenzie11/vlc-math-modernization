## Course description

This course builds on how we think about and model relationships — from simpler patterns to more complex ones — using **functions**. It extends students’ experiences with quadratic relations and focuses on **quadratic, trigonometric, and exponential** functions and their use in modelling real-world situations. Students represent functions numerically, graphically, and algebraically; simplify expressions; solve equations; and solve problems relating to applications. Students reason mathematically and communicate their thinking as they solve multi-step problems.

**Prerequisite:** Principles of Mathematics, Grade 10 Academic (MPM2D) or Foundations of Mathematics, Grade 10 Applied (MFM2P).

**Textbook:** *Functions and Applications 11* (Nelson) — access via the course textbook link in Canvas.

## How this course is built

Math is usually presented as a pile of techniques. This course is built around **stories of change** — in your life and in the models we use to describe the world — and the mathematics that makes those stories precise.

You will work through **five themed modules** (plus a short intro week):

| Module | Title | Rough focus |
|-------:|-------|-------------|
| 0 | Intro / housekeeping | Routines, tools, Honorlock, how assessment works |
| 1 | Change & Transformation | Quadratic thinking — rates, turning points, transformations |
| 2 | Trigonometry | Solving triangles; trig as a modelling tool |
| 3 | Waves | Periodic / sinusoidal functions |
| 4 | Explosions | Exponential growth and decay |
| 5 | Finance | Compound interest, annuities, and related models |

Each content module runs about **three weeks**. After Module 5 there is a **culminating / review** week before the exam window.

## Assessment and evaluation

| Component | Weight | What it is |
|-----------|-------:|------------|
| **Module assessments** (doc + conference) | **50%** | One major assessment per content module (Modules 1–5) |
| **Quizzes** | **20%** | Two Honorlock-proctored multiple-choice quizzes per content module |
| **Final exam** | **30%** | Cumulative; Honorlock-proctored |

Evaluation uses the four categories of the achievement chart (Knowledge & Understanding, Thinking, Communication, Application) as appropriate to each task.

### Module assessments (50%)

Each content module has a **story-based assessment** that you build out of **artifacts** — pieces of mathematical evidence — stitched into **one continuous document**.

Artifacts play the role that “math questions and answers” play on a typical assignment or test. Here, you create them from related problems or examples and weave them into your story as foundational connections.

Every artifact must include:

- **Model** — a graph, sketch, table, equation, or diagram that stands in for that specific change
- **Reasoning** — a few sentences explaining why that math represents what happened

Each module assessment also includes a **required one-on-one video conference**, where you walk through your story and defend the math. Missing the conference limits what can be confirmed, regardless of how strong the written document looks.

### Quizzes (20%)

- **Two multiple-choice quizzes per content module** (Modules 1–5)
- **Honorlock-proctored**; closed-book; completed individually
- Aligned to the same ideas as that module’s assessment
- Window and time limit are posted on each quiz in Canvas

### Final exam (30%)

- Cumulative across the course
- **Honorlock required**
- Details and review materials appear in the exam / review module near the end of term

## Collaboration and AI

**Collaboration:** You may discuss and brainstorm with peers. **Written work you submit must be your own** — your models, your reasoning, your words. Study groups are encouraged when individual accountability stays intact.

**AI:** Within a module, AI can be a **study companion and checking tool** — not a ghostwriter for your assessment doc. Treat AI output as something to **question**, not as an authority. Quizzes and the exam still require you to think without it.

**Academic honesty:** Plagiarism, cheating on quizzes/exams, submitting others’ work as your own, or using AI to replace your thinking on assessed work may result in a mark of zero and/or loss of online privileges under school policy.
