Change & Transformation

Life is full of change — sometimes steady, sometimes speeding up, slowing down, or flipping direction. This module builds the math that helps you describe those patterns clearly, so you can make better sense of the stories unfolding around you.

Relationships we can trust

Every day you use relationships without calling them that:

  • hours worked → pay
  • practice time → how a song feels
  • distance → time

In math we represent relationships so we can talk about them clearly — with a table, a graph, a mapping, or an equation. The point isn’t decoration. It’s understanding life a little more precisely.

Some relationships are messy (one input, many possible outputs). The ones you can rely on — each allowed input has one clear output — are especially useful. Later we’ll give those a special name.

Try this

Think of something that changed for you this week. What was the input? What was the output? If two people used your description, would they get the same output for the same input?

A2.3

When change itself changes

Some change is steady: the same bump every equal step. That’s constant change — what you’ve seen in straight-line relationships.

Other change speeds up or slows down. The simplest pattern like that in this course is a curve that bends like a parabola. You’ll spot it in tables by looking at first differences (and when those aren’t constant, second differences).

We’ll also talk carefully about which inputs make sense and which outputs show up — in context and on a graph.

A1.1

Seeing curved change in real life

Before we pile on formal labels, practice seeing non-constant change in life: area growing with a side length, a ball’s height over time, practice that accelerates, a situation that speeds up then slows.

You’ll also meet a clean shorthand for “plug this input into the relationship” — once the idea of a reliable relationship is already clear. That shorthand is function notation.

Suppose \(f\) is the rule “double the input, then add 1.” We write \(f(x)=2x+1\). Important: \(f(x)\) does not mean “\(f\) times \(x\).” It means “the output of rule \(f\) when the input is \(x\).”

To evaluate, replace every \(x\) with the number you care about:

  • \(f(3)=2(3)+1=7\)
  • \(f(0)=1\)
  • \(f(-2)=-3\)

Same idea with a life label: \(H(t)\) for height at time \(t\). Then \(H(2)=7\) means “when the input is 2, the height is 7” — just “plug 2 into the height rule.” The Explore and example below give you more practice plugging values in.

Think it through

Pick something from your life (sport, music, gaming, a habit, a friendship, a job). Where did the speed of change stay steady? Where did that speed itself change? Describe each in plain language first — two short sentences are enough. You can attach the math words later once the idea is clear.

A1.1

Quadratic Equations, 3 Ways

A quadratic relationship is the same curve no matter how you write the equation. What changes is which features sit in plain sight. We call these writings the three quadratic forms:

  • Standard form \(y=ax^2+bx+c\) — the constant term \(c\) is the \(y\)-intercept (the output when \(x=0\)).
  • Factored form \(y=a(x-r)(x-s)\) — the values \(r\) and \(s\) are the zeros (also called the \(x\)-intercepts), where the graph crosses the \(x\)-axis.
  • Vertex form \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\) — the point \((h,k)\) is the vertex (the peak or low point of the parabola).

First you will learn to recognize each form from how the equation is written. Then you will practice reading the features each form makes easy — including which inputs and outputs make sense when a story restricts them.

Try this

Look at \(y=2(x-3)^2+5\), \(y=(x+1)(x-4)\), and \(y=x^2-6x+8\). Before you expand or graph anything: which is vertex form? which is factored? which is standard? Write the three labels, then check yourself in the examples below.

Recognizing the three forms

Before you dig into features, practice a quieter skill: name the form from the equation’s shape.

Look for the pattern:

  • Powers written out as \(ax^2+bx+c\) → standard form
  • A product of linear factors \(a(x-r)(x-s)\) → factored form
  • A squared binomial plus a constant \(a(x-h)^2+k\) → vertex form

Same parabola underneath — different algebraic packaging. Naming the form tells you which features you can read immediately.

Vertex form

In vertex form \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\), the turning point of the parabola — its peak or low point — is written into the equation as the vertex \((h,k)\).

The sign of \(a\) tells you whether that vertex is a maximum (opens down) or a minimum (opens up). Watch the Explore resources, then read vertices, ranges, and max/min values from equations written this way.

A2.9

Standard form

Standard form \(y=ax^2+bx+c\) is the expanded polynomial look. The constant \(c\) is immediate: it is the \(y\)-intercept — the output when \(x=0\).

The leading coefficient \(a\) still tells opening direction and steepness, but the vertex and zeros usually take more work from this writing alone. That is why you keep the other forms nearby.

A1.2 · A2.4

Algebra of Converting Quadratic Forms

The same curved relationship can answer different questions from life: when does something hit zero? where is the peak? what happens at the start? Each algebraic writing makes a different part of that story easy to see. Moving between forms is how you match the model to the question you are actually asking.

Try this

You’re given \(y=x^2-6x+5\) and you want the vertex. Will you complete the square, or factor and use the midpoint of the zeros? Why?

Expanding

You’ve been expanding for a while now — multiplying binomials, clearing brackets, writing products as ordinary polynomials. We’re staying with it here on purpose: expanding is a key useful skill, and it’s the natural starting point for everything that follows. Factoring is expanding in reverse, and later rewrites (toward vertex form) lean on being comfortable moving between a product and \(ax^2+bx+c\).

Expanding is the “multiply it out” move. When a curved relationship is written as a product — factored form, or a squared piece in vertex form — expanding unpacks it into the familiar \(y=ax^2+bx+c\) look.

That’s useful when you want the starting height (the \(y\)-intercept) or when a tool expects the ordinary polynomial shape. Expanding doesn’t change the story; it just shows a different page of the same book.

Factoring

Factoring is expanding’s reverse: you start from a polynomial and rebuild it as a product. Why care? Factored form makes the zeros obvious — the inputs where the output hits zero. In life stories, those zeros are often the moments something lands, empties, or breaks even.

Most factoring mistakes aren’t “bad algebra” so much as skipping a decision. Use the flowchart below as a deliberate checklist, not a magic spell:

Always ask first: is there a common factor? Pulling a GCF out front makes everything else smaller and cleaner — like simplifying a recipe before you cook.

Then count the terms. Two terms often means difference of squares: \(a^2-b^2=(a-b)(a+b)\). Three terms? Check for a perfect-square trinomial, then a simple \(x^2+bx+c\) pair search (numbers that multiply to \(c\) and add to \(b\)), then harder cases with decomposition/grouping when the leading coefficient isn’t 1. Four terms often point to grouping.

Not every expression factors nicely over the integers you know — and that’s okay. The flowchart’s job is to help you try the right tool before you assume you’re stuck. Watch the Explore resources, then work the examples with the chart open beside you.

Converting between forms · Completing the square

Sometimes the question you care about isn’t “when does this hit zero?” — it’s “where’s the peak or low point?” Vertex form \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\) answers that immediately: the vertex is \((h,k)\). Completing the square is the purposeful rewrite that gets you there from standard form.

Here’s the core idea, patiently: you rearrange \(x^2+bx\) by adding and subtracting \((b/2)^2\) so a perfect square appears. That isn’t a trick for its own sake — you’re building the squared chunk that sits at the heart of vertex form. When \(a\neq 1\), factor \(a\) out of the \(x\) terms first, complete the square inside, then distribute carefully.

There’s a second conversion path that lives next to completing the square: symmetry. If you already know two zeros, the axis of symmetry is halfway between them, and that axis is the vertex’s \(x\)-coordinate. Factor → midpoint of zeros → evaluate for the \(y\)-coordinate. Same destination as completing the square, different road.

Choose on purpose. Need zeros? Factor. Need the turning point and you don’t have clean zeros? Complete the square. Have zeros already? Symmetry may be faster. Either way, you’re not collecting techniques for a test checklist — you’re choosing the writing that makes the life question (peak, landing, start) easier to see.

The Explore below walks completing the square; the examples practice both routes so you can match the method to the question you’re actually asking.

Stretch, flip, shift

Sometimes a story doesn’t invent a brand-new pattern — it takes a familiar curve and stretches it, flips it, or slides it.

In vertex form \(y=a(x-h)^2+k\), the numbers \(a\), \(h\), and \(k\) describe those moves relative to \(y=x^2\). That’s useful in math class and in telling a before/after story from life.
